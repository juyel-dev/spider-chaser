A slow walk through the autumn garden

Garden notes · 6 min read

By the second week of October the garden has settled into its quiet routine. The tomato vines are bare, the last marigolds hold on by the fence, and the mornings smell of wet leaves and cold soil. It is the best time of year to just walk around with a cup of tea and look closely at things.

What to look for

Start low. The beds that looked finished in summer are full of small activity: beetles working through the mulch, snails parked under the lip of a flowerpot, and early morning dew sitting on every thread and stem. Dew is the easiest way to see how many webs a garden really has — a hedge that seemed empty at noon turns into a field of tiny silver bowls at sunrise.

Move on to the fence line. Old wood collects moss on the north side and warmth on the south, and the two sides host completely different neighbours. The sunny boards are crowded with snails and ladybirds waiting out the cold, while the damp side keeps woodlice and centipedes.

Letting things be

It is tempting to tidy everything before winter. Cut back the dead stems, rake every leaf, clear the corners. But most of what lives in a garden spends the cold months inside those stems, leaf piles and corners. Leaving a few untidy patches costs nothing and gives the whole place somewhere to survive until spring.

A good compromise is to tidy the paths and the edges, where you walk, and leave the back of the beds alone. By March, when the first warm afternoons arrive, the garden will already be waking up without any help from you.

A small checklist

Before the first frost: bring the tender pots inside, empty the water butt a little so it does not crack, collect seeds from the flowers you want next year, and write down what worked. Notes made now save a lot of guessing in spring, and the garden will look different again by then.
